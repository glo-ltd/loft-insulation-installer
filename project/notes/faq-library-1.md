## Homepage — Loft Insulation Installer FAQs

Q: What is loft insulation?
A: Loft insulation is a layer of thermally resistant material installed in the roof space to slow the escape of heat. It works by trapping pockets of still air, dramatically slowing the transfer of warmth from the heated rooms below. Because roughly a quarter of a home's heat is lost upward in an uninsulated property, this simple barrier has a large effect on comfort and running costs. Options include natural sheep's wool and hemp, recycled plastic and reflective multifoil. Correctly installed to around 270mm, or the equivalent for higher-performance products, it keeps working for decades.

Q: What is the purpose of loft insulation?
A: To slow the escape of heat through the roof so the rooms below stay warmer, more comfortable and more efficient to heat. A properly installed layer creates a thermal barrier across the loft floor or, in some projects, along the roof rafters. Rooms warm up faster, stay warm for longer and feel less affected by draughts or cold spots. In summer, certain systems, especially foil at rafter level, can also help reduce heat build-up. A good installation improves comfort without creating condensation problems.

Q: How is loft insulation installed?
A: It begins with a survey of the loft's dimensions, existing insulation, ventilation, joist depth and any hazards. For a standard cold loft, the first layer is laid snugly between the ceiling joists and a second layer across them at right angles to eliminate cold bridging, building up to around 270mm. Installers keep the eaves clear so air can circulate, insulate pipes and tanks separately, and avoid compressing the material. The job is finished tidily, with old or damaged insulation removed and bagged. A typical three-bedroom house takes just a few hours.

Q: How long does loft insulation installation take?
A: A standard three-bedroom house with reasonable access can usually be insulated in two to four hours, and most installations are completed within a single day. Timing depends on the size and layout of the loft, how easy it is to access, and the condition of any existing insulation. Clearing out old material adds time, as does combining insulation with extras such as boarding, a new hatch or ventilation improvements. Your surveyor will give you a realistic timescale alongside your quote.

Q: How much does loft insulation cost to install in the UK?
A: It depends on the size of the loft, the material chosen, the depth required, the condition of any existing insulation and how easy the space is to access. As a broad guide, a professional installation can range from several hundred pounds to over one thousand pounds. Compare what is included, not just the headline price: a complete quote covers the survey, preparation, ventilation protection, pipe insulation where needed, and tidy disposal. Recycled plastic is often a strong value option; sheep wool and hemp are premium natural materials.

Q: Is loft insulation worth the investment?
A: For the overwhelming majority of homes, yes. It is consistently rated among the most cost-effective energy improvements: relatively inexpensive, quick to install and delivering savings every year for decades. With around a quarter of heat escaping through an uninsulated roof, the reduction in wasted energy is immediate, and standard insulation lasts 40 years or more. Add a warmer home with fewer draughts, a lower carbon footprint and an improved EPC rating, and the case is strong, provided the work is done properly.

Q: Can I add loft insulation to an existing property?
A: Almost certainly, yes. The vast majority of existing UK homes can have loft insulation added or topped up. If your loft is uninsulated, a full layer is laid from scratch; if it has older 50mm-100mm material, a top-up brings it to today's 270mm standard. Degraded, damp or contaminated material is cleared out first. Breathable sheep's wool or hemp suit older homes, foil suits mid-century houses and recycled plastic suits newer builds, with the final choice confirmed on site during your free survey.

Q: Are your loft insulation installers accredited and trustworthy?
A: We connect you with professional installers who survey the property, explain the options and provide a clear specification before work begins. A trustworthy installer explains the proposed work in plain English: what material, how deep, whether existing insulation is topped up or removed, and how the eaves stay clear. Expect a written quote, tidy workmanship, insurance and workmanship guarantees. The right installer looks at the building first, then recommends the option that best balances warmth, comfort, safety, access and value.

Q: What should I look for in loft insulation installers near me?
A: Look beyond the cheapest price to the quality of the survey, the clarity of the specification and the care taken with ventilation. A good installer measures the loft properly, checks existing insulation depth, inspects access, and explains the material options in plain English rather than pushing one product. The quote should state the material, depth, area covered, preparation work, disposal and how ventilation will be protected. Compressed, patchy or badly laid insulation will not perform, so workmanship matters as much as price.

Q: What is the difference between installers, fitters, companies and specialists?
A: A fitter is usually the person physically installing the insulation; an installer may be the individual or team carrying out the work; a company is the business responsible for the survey, quote, installation and aftercare; and a specialist brings deeper knowledge of materials, roof structures, ventilation and storage. For a homeowner, the label matters less than the process. Choose the team that can explain why a material suits your loft, what depth is needed and how the work avoids common problems.

Q: Can you help with insulation, boarding, ladders and hatches together?
A: Yes, and the best loft projects often combine them, because these elements affect one another. If boards are fixed directly over joists, deep insulation gets compressed and loses performance; a raised boarding system creates a platform above the insulation instead. A properly fitted ladder and insulated hatch make the space easier to access while reducing draughts. One survey covering the whole loft avoids paying twice, prevents awkward retrofitting and produces a neater, better-performing result.

## Loft Insulation hub — Loft Insulation FAQs

Q: How much loft insulation do I need?
A: Enough depth to hit the modern thermal target: current UK guidance recommends around 270mm of mineral or natural wool to achieve a roof U-value of about 0.16 W/m²K, with 250mm the commonly cited minimum. Older properties were often fitted with just 50mm-100mm, so many homes benefit from a top-up. High-performance products such as multifoil can reach the same U-value at a thinner depth. Your surveyor measures the loft and recommends precisely how much your property needs.

Q: How thick should loft insulation be in the UK?
A: Current best practice is around 270mm of mineral or natural wool across the loft floor, delivering the recommended U-value of approximately 0.16 W/m²K. Dense high-performance boards or reflective multifoil can achieve the target at reduced thickness. When topping up, insulation is laid in two layers, the second across the joists at right angles to eliminate cold bridging. Don't over-compress the material or block the eaves, and note that going much beyond 270mm brings diminishing returns.

Q: Can you have too much loft insulation?
A: You cannot make your home too warm, but beyond roughly 270mm each additional layer saves progressively less, so extra material rarely justifies its cost. The bigger consideration is airflow: a well-insulated loft floor makes the loft above colder, so without ventilation, warm moist air rising from the house can condense on the timbers. Insulation should never block the eaves vents, should not be compressed, and tanks and pipes should be insulated separately. Balanced insulation and ventilation is the goal.

Q: Which type of loft insulation do I need?
A: It depends on your property's age, construction and your priorities. Period and pre-1920 homes with untreated timbers suit breathable materials such as sheep's wool or hemp. Properties from roughly 1920-1965 pair well with reflective multifoil. Newer, post-1965 homes are well served by soft, easy-to-handle recycled plastic. These are starting points rather than rules; vegan preferences, allergies and storage plans all matter too. Your surveyor will recommend the material that delivers the best performance and value for your home.

Q: Which loft insulation type is best overall?
A: There is no single best material. Foil is versatile, heat-reflective and ideal for mid-century properties. Sheep's wool is long-lasting, breathable and fully organic, the natural choice for period homes. Recycled plastic is durable, affordable, safe to handle and highly sustainable in modern houses. Hemp offers natural breathability with a vegan profile and an exceptionally long lifespan. All comfortably outperform older fibreglass. The deciding factors are your property's age and construction, breathability, budget and environmental preferences.

Q: How much energy and money can I save with loft insulation?
A: An uninsulated roof loses around a quarter of a home's heat, so the reduction in wasted energy is immediate. The carbon savings are just as compelling: a detached home can cut around 1,000kg of CO2 a year, a semi-detached about 600kg and a mid-terrace roughly 550kg. Actual savings depend on your home's size, current insulation, heating and energy prices. For a tailored estimate, our network installers can model it during a no-obligation survey.

Q: How often should loft insulation be replaced?
A: Rarely. Standard mineral wool typically lasts 40 years or more if it stays dry and undisturbed; sheep's wool is often cited at up to 60 years and hemp up to a century. Insulation usually only needs attention if it has been damaged, compressed, contaminated or soaked by a leak. If older insulation is thinner than today's 270mm standard, a top-up rather than full replacement is the most common course. If you're unsure of its condition, a free survey will tell you.

Q: Can loft insulation stop condensation in the loft?
A: It helps when installed as part of a properly ventilated roof space, but it is not a standalone cure. Condensation happens when warm, moist air meets cold surfaces. A good installation keeps the eaves clear so air can carry moisture away, avoids stuffing insulation into tight roof edges, and seals the loft hatch. If condensation is already present, the installer should check for blocked ventilation, roof leaks and extraction issues first. When insulation and airflow are balanced, damp and mould are far less likely.

Q: Should old loft insulation be removed first?
A: Not always. If it is dry, clean, evenly laid and uncontaminated, it can often stay and be topped up to the right depth, with a fresh layer laid across the joists at right angles. Removal is recommended when the existing material is damp, mouldy, heavily compressed, contaminated by pests or full of debris, since poor material hides problems and reduces the new layer's effectiveness. The decision should follow an inspection rather than age alone.

Q: Can I insulate between roof rafters instead of the loft floor?
A: In some lofts, yes, but it serves a different purpose. Loft floor insulation creates a cold loft and is usually the most cost-effective way to cut heat loss. Rafter-level insulation creates a warmer roof space, useful for an insulated storage room or temperature-sensitive stored items. Done badly it can trap damp against roof timbers, so a survey should assess roof structure, ventilation, access and boarding before recommending joist-level, rafter-level or combined insulation.

## Sheep Wool page — Sheep wool loft insulation FAQs

Q: What is sheep wool loft insulation?
A: A natural insulation made from sheep's fleece, processed into rolls or batts laid in the roof space. After shearing, the wool is cleaned, dried and treated with mild agents to improve fire resistance and deter moths. Its crimped fibres trap air, giving thermal performance comparable to, and in damp conditions often better than, mineral wool or fibreglass. It is breathable, moisture-regulating, renewable, biodegradable, sound-absorbing and safe to handle, with a higher price than basic fibreglass offset by a very long lifespan.

Q: What are the benefits of sheep wool loft insulation?
A: Its crimped fibres trap thousands of tiny air pockets, keeping homes warm in winter and cooler in summer. It can absorb and release up to around a third of its own weight in water vapour without losing performance, making it outstanding at regulating humidity and protecting roof timbers. It is renewable and fully biodegradable, naturally fire-resistant, an effective acoustic insulator, and treated to resist moths and mould. There are no sharp fibres, so it is kind to skin and lungs, and it even absorbs minor airborne pollutants.

Q: Can you insulate a house with sheep wool?
A: Yes, very effectively. Sheep wool is laid between and across the ceiling joists, or used as part of a carefully designed insulated loft space, trapping air within the natural crimp of the fibres. It is especially appealing in older and character properties because it is breathable and manages moisture without losing its insulating structure. The main trade-off is cost, but many homeowners choose it for the natural material, comfort benefits and premium finish. A survey confirms it is right for your loft.

Q: Is sheep's wool insulation better than fibreglass?
A: It has clear advantages. Fibreglass sheds sharp, easily inhaled fibres that demand masks and gloves; wool is soft, skin-friendly and safe to handle. Thermally the two are broadly similar, but wool pulls ahead in real UK conditions because it keeps performing when damp, while fibreglass can lose much of its insulating value. Wool is also breathable, fire-resistant, sound-absorbing, renewable and biodegradable. Its drawback is upfront cost, offset by a lifespan commonly cited at up to 60 years.

Q: How thick should sheep's wool insulation be?
A: The target is a roof U-value of about 0.16 W/m²K, which generally means around 270mm of sheep's wool when insulating from scratch, built up in layers: the first between the joists, further layers across them at right angles to avoid cold bridging. If your loft already has sound insulation, a top-up layer of perhaps 120mm-160mm may be enough. Wool should be laid loosely, never compressed, with ventilation preserved at the eaves. Your surveyor will specify the exact depth.

Q: How long does sheep wool loft insulation last?
A: Exceptionally long: commonly around 60 years and often regarded as lasting the lifetime of the building. Wool fibre holds its shape and loft over time rather than settling, resists fire, is treated to deter moths and vermin, and manages moisture extremely well without rotting. In normal circumstances you will not need to replace it; the main exceptions are major events such as a significant roof leak or physical damage. Occasional checks are still sensible.

Q: Is sheep wool insulation sustainable?
A: It is one of the most sustainable insulation materials available. Wool regrows with each shearing, much of it sourced from British farms, requires relatively little energy to process, and is fully biodegradable at the end of its long life. By insulating effectively for decades it also cuts the heating energy and emissions of the home year after year. It contains no harmful VOCs. Some products are treated with mild fire- and pest-resistant agents or blended with a small share of other fibres, so check the specification if pure natural content matters.

Q: Is sheep wool insulation flammable?
A: No, wool is naturally fire-resistant. It has a high ignition threshold and, rather than bursting into flame, it chars and self-extinguishes, because its protein structure and natural moisture content resist combustion and it needs far more oxygen to sustain a flame than many synthetics. Commercial products are additionally treated to meet recognised fire standards. As with all insulation, correct installation matters, keeping material clear of recessed downlights and hot flues, which our installers do as standard.

Q: How does sheep wool loft insulation work?
A: Through the same principle as the fleece on a living sheep: it traps air. Each fibre has a natural crimp, and layered together the fibres create millions of tiny pockets of still air, which dramatically slow the movement of warmth from the rooms below. The same structure cushions sound. Wool's fibres are also hygroscopic, absorbing water vapour when humidity is high and releasing it as the air dries, without losing thermal performance, keeping the loft balanced and protecting roof timbers.

Q: How is sheep wool loft insulation made?
A: Wool is collected by routine shearing, with much of it sourced from British farms, then cleaned to remove dirt and lanolin, dried, and treated with mild, low-toxicity agents for fire resistance and pest protection. The fibres are then sorted, blended, carded and bonded into thick, lofty quilts, before being compressed into rolls for transport and installation. Because it is a renewable resource needing relatively little processing energy, the finished product has a low embodied carbon footprint.

Q: How does sheep wool compare to other types of insulation?
A: It matches mineral wool and fibreglass thermally, with a real edge in damp UK lofts because it keeps performing when moist. Against the other eco materials: recycled plastic offers strong all-round performance at a lower price; hemp shares wool's breathable, moisture-regulating qualities and is vegan with an exceptionally long lifespan; multifoil is thin and versatile where space is limited. Wool's drawbacks are cost and slightly more careful handling. For older, damp-prone or heritage homes it is frequently the best fit.

Q: Is sheep wool loft insulation safe?
A: One of the safest materials to live with and install. It does not shed sharp fibres, so it does not irritate skin, eyes or lungs and can be handled without heavy protective equipment. It releases no harmful VOCs, helps regulate humidity, and is naturally fire-resistant, charring and self-extinguishing rather than spreading flame. Pest treatments are mild and low-toxicity. It is often favoured by people with allergies or sensitivities to traditional insulation.
